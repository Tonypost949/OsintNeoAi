Business and Economy of Mexico
Public business registries in Mexico disclose incorporation details and ownership structures that support corporate profiling. Analysts routinely consult financial disclosure requirements to assess operational transparency of registered entities. Understanding common legal forms helps interpret available records more effectively.
⬛ Forms of ownership and business
Persona Física con Actividad Empresarial
– An individual registered with the tax authority (SAT) to conduct commercial activities without forming a separate legal entity; unlimited personal liability.
Sociedad de Responsabilidad Limitada (S. de R.L. de C.V.)
– A limited liability company formed by one or more partners; liability is restricted to the value of contributions.
Sociedad Anónima (S.A. de C.V.)
– A stock company whose capital is divided into shares; may be publicly listed (S.A.B. de C.V.) or privately held; shareholders’ liability is limited to their shareholding.
Sociedad por Acciones Simplificada (S.A.S.)
– A simplified stock company allowing single-founder incorporation and flexible governance; suitable for startups and SMEs.
Sociedad Civil (S.C.)
– A civil partnership used primarily for professional services; partners retain unlimited liability.
Unidad Económica / Régimen de Incorporación Fiscal (RIF)
– Simplified tax regimes for micro-enterprises and self-employed individuals with reduced compliance requirements.
Non-profit entities (A.C., I.A.P., etc.)
– Associations and foundations registered for social, cultural or charitable purposes without profit distribution.
⬛ How business is registered
Commercial companies are incorporated before a notary public and subsequently registered in the Registro Público de Comercio (RPC) via the Sistema Integral de Gestión Registral (SIGER) operated by the Ministry of Economy.
The tax identification number (RFC) is obtained automatically through the SAT portal after incorporation or via the “Inscripción al RFC” procedure for individuals.
Online filing is available through gob.mx, e.firma (electronic signature) and the Portal de trámites del SAT; in-person registration is also possible at SAT offices or via state-level one-stop shops.
Required documents for companies typically include the notarized deed of incorporation, proof of legal domicile, identification of founders and administrators, and payment of registration fees.
Economic activities are classified according to the Sistema de Clasificación Industrial de América del Norte (SCIAN), the Mexican adaptation of NAICS.
⬛ What is published publicly
The Registro Público de Comercio (RPC) and the SAT public consultation portal disclose the company’s full legal name, commercial name, RFC, date of incorporation, legal form and current status (active, suspended, cancelled).
Public records list the registered address, names of legal representatives and administrators, share capital amount, and the principal and secondary SCIAN activity codes.
Changes to corporate data (address, management, capital modifications) are recorded as historical entries and remain accessible through the RPC search interface.
Information on licences, permits and certain regulatory authorisations is available via sector-specific registries (e.g., COFEPRIS, CNBV) when applicable.
Bankruptcy, liquidation and merger notices are published in the Diario Oficial de la Federación and reflected in the RPC status field.
⬛ Availability of financial reports
Most private companies file financial statements only with the SAT and are not required to publish them publicly.
Listed companies (S.A.B. de C.V.) and certain regulated entities must submit audited financial statements to the Bolsa Mexicana de Valores (BMV) and the Comisión Nacional Bancaria y de Valores (CNBV), where summaries are publicly accessible.
Large taxpayers and companies exceeding statutory thresholds must file annual financial information electronically with the SAT; these filings are not released to the general public.
Credit bureaus and commercial databases aggregate limited financial indicators derived from public filings and payment behaviour, but full balance sheets remain restricted.
These economic data points collectively enable robust due-diligence workflows focused on Mexican commercial actors.

: English editions or sections exist in major outlets (El Universal, Milenio) for international audiences. Indigenous-language publications are minimal and mostly non-digital.
⬛ Censorship and Press Freedom
Repression level
: Mexico ranks 121st out of 180 in RSF’s 2024 World Press Freedom Index, classified as a “difficult situation”.
Violence against journalists
: Mexico remains one of the most dangerous countries for journalists, with frequent killings and threats, especially in states with organized crime presence.
Legal environment
: No formal military censorship; however, criminal defamation laws and strategic lawsuits are used against critical reporting.
Access issues
: Most national and regional outlets remain publicly accessible; independent digital media operate without systematic blocking.
These media resources therefore enrich contextual understanding in Mexico-focused open-source investigations.

Limitations and Legal Framework in Mexico
Mexican data protection legislation defines clear boundaries around personal information that researchers must respect during collection. Publicly available records remain accessible, yet any processing must align with applicable privacy statutes and platform terms. Analysts maintain compliance by restricting activities to explicitly open and lawfully published materials.
⬛ What is considered personal data
*
Federal Law on the Protection of Personal Data Held by Private Parties (LFPDPPP, 2010, as amended)
– Regulates the lawful processing, storage, and transfer of personal data by private entities.
*
Personal data
– Any information that identifies or can be used to identify an individual (full name, CURP, address, telephone, email, IP address, geolocation, tax ID).
*
Sensitive personal data
– Information concerning racial or ethnic origin, health status, genetic information, religious beliefs, political opinions, sexual orientation, and biometric data used for identification.
*
ARCO rights
– Legal rights of access, rectification, cancellation, and opposition granted to data subjects.
⬛ What is allowed to search
*
Federal Law on Transparency and Access to Public Information (2015)
– Establishes the right to access government-held information and open public registries.
*
Public state registries
(Public Registry of Commerce, property records, official gazettes, court decisions published in compliance with transparency rules).
*
Open government data portals
(datos.gob.mx and state-level open data platforms).
*
Publicly available information
voluntarily disclosed on websites, social media, professional directories, and media sources.
*
Official publications
and statistical datasets released by government agencies.
*
Data accessed in accordance with platform terms of service
and applicable licensing conditions.
*
Anonymized or aggregated datasets
that do not permit individual identification.
⬛ What is prohibited to search
*
LFPDPPP
– Prohibits processing of personal data without a legal basis or the data subject’s consent, especially sensitive personal data.
*
Federal Penal Code, Article 211 bis
– Criminalizes unauthorized access to computer systems or protected data.
*
Acquisition, purchase, or use of leaked databases
containing personal information obtained unlawfully.
*
Collection of restricted information
through hacking, social engineering, or circumvention of access controls.
*
Processing of sensitive personal data
without explicit consent or another lawful basis.
⬛ Liability for abuse
*
LFPDPPP and INAI sanctions
– Administrative fines for unlawful processing or inadequate protection of personal data.
*
Federal Penal Code, Article 211 bis
– Criminal penalties including fines and imprisonment for unauthorized access to computer information.
*
Civil liability
– Obligation to compensate data subjects for material or moral damages resulting from unlawful processing.
*
Regulatory measures
– Blocking or restriction of online resources that violate data protection or transparency legislation.
These legal parameters ultimately safeguard both the integrity of research and the rights of individuals referenced in Mexican open sources.
