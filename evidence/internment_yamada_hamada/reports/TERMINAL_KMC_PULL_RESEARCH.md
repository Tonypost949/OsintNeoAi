## Q1 — Terminal Island, California — DOCUMENTED

**Source: Densho Encyclopedia, "Terminal Island, California"** (opened in full; page last updated Mar 23, 2026)
https://encyclopedia.densho.org/wiki/Terminal_Island,_California

Documented facts from that source:

- Terminal Island held a Japanese fishing village at East San Pedro, Port of Los Angeles.
- First Japanese settlers were abalone/lobster fishermen in 1899; the community shifted to East San Pedro by 1910 (sardines/tuna).
- Peak population was **about 3,000 in the 1930s**.
- Every resident was connected to fishing / canneries (named in the source: Van Camp, White Star).
- After December 7, 1941: FBI carried out widespread arrests of Issei leaders/fishermen (Category A "known dangerous," FBI Custodial Detention Index / ABC Lists); the Navy searched homes.
- Executive Order 9066 was signed February 19, 1942.
- **February 25, 1942: Navy personnel posted notices mandating all Japanese Americans (aliens and native-born citizens) off the island within 48 hours.**
- Terminal Island residents were the **first to be evicted** — less than a week after EO 9066.
- Interim temporary quarters followed; residents were given the opportunity to move to Manzanar on April 1, 1942.
- The Navy occupied East San Pedro, razed homes/shops, and confiscated boats.
- A Densho Encyclopedia entry on Herbert Nicholson adds: 500 fishing families given 48 hours.

**Supporting source: City of Los Angeles, Council District 15 (83rd anniversary motion)**
https://cd15.lacity.gov/news/council-takes-step-forward-designate-historic-japanese-american-fishing-village-historic

- First community in the country forcibly removed.
- Village home to over 3,000.
- Last two surviving buildings at 700-702 and 712-716 Tuna Street.

**Supporting source: Calisphere / Online Archive of California — finding aid, Terminal Island Personal Histories Collection**
https://oac.cdlib.org/findaid/ark:/13030/c8kw5p9z/

## Q2 — The freedom-of-movement paper trail — DOCUMENTED

### Executive Order 9066 — February 19, 1942

- **EO 9066**, February 19, 1942, F.R. Doc. 42–1563: authorized the Secretary of War / military commanders to prescribe military areas "from which any or all persons may be excluded" and to restrict the right "to enter, remain in, or leave."
- National Archives (NARA) summary: EO 9066 authorized forced removal of all persons deemed a threat from the West Coast to inland "relocation centers," resulting in the incarceration of Japanese Americans.

**Source: NARA, Prologue magazine (RG 210 article)**
https://www.archives.gov/publications/prologue/2009/winter/wra.html

Documented from that source:

- The Army forcibly evacuated Japanese Americans late March – May 1942.
- Beginning May 1942, evacuees were transferred to **10 War Relocation Authority (WRA) centers**: Central Utah, Colorado River, Gila River, Granada, Heart Mountain, Jerome, Manzanar, Minidoka, Rohwer, Tule Lake.
- Some remained until the centers closed in 1946.
- **WRA was established March 18, 1942, by EO 9102.**

**Source: NARA, RG 210 series "Notices, Proclamations, and Laws, Feb 20, 1942–1945" (NAID 5634025)**, described at:
https://www.archives.gov/research/aapi/ww2/planning

- That series holds Western Defense Command / WRA executive orders, proclamations, and exclusion orders.
- Public Law 77-503 (NAID 299811) made violating EO 9066 restrictions a misdemeanor (up to 1 year / $5,000).

### Western Defense Command proclamations

- **Public Proclamation No. 1, March 2, 1942** (Lt. Gen. J. L. DeWitt): designated Military Areas Nos. 1 and 2 (western WA/OR/CA, southern AZ).
- **Public Proclamation No. 2, March 16, 1942**: designated Areas 3–6.
- **Public Proclamation No. 3, March 24, 1942 — curfew:** effective 6:00 a.m. March 27, 1942 — all Japanese/German/Italian aliens and all persons of Japanese ancestry in Military Area No. 1 were to be within their residence **8:00 p.m.–6:00 a.m.**, to be only at residence or place of employment otherwise, and travel was limited to **no more than 5 miles from residence**. After March 31, 1942, a contraband list applied (firearms, cameras, short-wave radios, etc.).

Primary text held at California State University, Dominguez Hills:
http://digitalcollections.archives.csudh.edu/digital/api/collection/p16855coll4/id/12133/download

Corroboration in Densho Digital Repository material (Minoru Yasui testimony; *Hirabayashi v. United States*, 828 F.2d 591).

### Civilian Exclusion Orders — 108 orders

**Source: Densho Encyclopedia, "Civilian exclusion orders"**
http://encyclopedia.densho.org/wiki/Civilian_exclusion_orders

Documented from that source:

- **108 Civilian Exclusion Orders** were issued late March – August 1942, excluding "all persons of Japanese ancestry, including aliens and non-aliens," neighborhood by neighborhood.
- Order No. 1 = Bainbridge Island, March 24, 1942.
- By June 6, 1942, all persons of Japanese ancestry had been removed from Military Area No. 1.
- The total of 108 includes Military Area No. 2 orders; removal was completed August 18, 1942.
- Densho's "Assembly centers" entry: the 108 Civilian Exclusion Orders uprooted **110,442 persons**.
- The Western Defense Command rescinded exclusion by **Public Proclamation No. 21, December 17, 1944**.

### Restriction inside the centers

**Civilian Restrictive Order No. 1, May 19, 1942** (via UC Berkeley / Bancroft Library digital collections):
https://digicoll.lib.berkeley.edu/record/175428/files/cubanc_35_1_00257638ta.pdf

- Persons of Japanese ancestry in assembly / reception / relocation centers were required to remain within bounds unless authorized — a direct freedom-of-movement restriction inside the centers themselves.

## Q3 — "KMC" — DOCUMENTED meaning found (Hawai'i, not California)

**Finding:** "KMC" is documented in authoritative sources as **Kīlauea Military Camp**, Hawai'i Volcanoes National Park — a U.S. Army internment camp for Japanese (and Japanese American) detainees in World War II. That expansion is stated in exactly those initials by the National Park Service, Densho, the Hawai'i state Act 163 report, the Japanese Cultural Center of Hawai'i / Farrell NPS-assisted overview, and U.S. Army / U.S. Senate sources.

**It is NOT documented in any source found in this pull as connected to Terminal Island, California fishing, Huntington Beach, or any Yamada.** That specific connection remains **NOT FOUND** (searches listed at the end of this section).

### Sources

**National Park Service, "Japanese Detention at Hawai'i Volcanoes"**
https://www.nps.gov/havo/learn/historyculture/japanese-detention.htm

- "More than 100 resident Japanese were interned at Kilauea Military Camp in World War II."
- A quiet military recreation facility became a detainment camp by the end of December 7, 1941; first detainees arrived the afternoon of December 7, 1941.
- ~2,000 persons (⅓ U.S. citizens) were incarcerated from Hawai'i.
- First group of **106 transferred from KMC to O'ahu on February 20, 1942** (per this NPS page — see date discrepancy note below).
- Last group of 25 transferred May 7, 1942 (per this NPS page).

**National Park Service, Honouliuli National Historic Site, "Hawaii's Other Internment Camps"**
https://www.nps.gov/hono/learn/historyculture/hawaii-s-other-internment-camps.htm

- KMC opened the same day Pearl Harbor was bombed.
- It held up to 100 resident Japanese at a time.
- It was the largest incarceration site in Hawai'i outside O'ahu.
- A total of 1,330 Japanese American citizens and Japanese nationals were incarcerated across Hawai'i (figure as stated on this page).

**Densho Encyclopedia, "Kilauea Military Camp (detention facility)"**
http://encyclopedia.densho.org/Kilauea Military Camp (detention facility)

- Facility type: U.S. Army Internment Camp, Kīlauea, Hawai'i; established 1916.
- FBI memo, March 26, 1942: 59 aliens and 20 citizens held there.
- Largest site in Hawai'i outside O'ahu.
- A POW camp was built at KMC in 1944 (Okinawan / possibly Korean POWs).

**Hawai'i Department of Land and Natural Resources report to the 24th Legislature under Act 163, SLH 2007 (November 2007)**

- Five known Hawai'i internment camps = Honouliuli, Sand Island, **Kilauea Military Camp (KMC)**, Haiku, Kalaheo Stockade.
- Six structures at KMC in which Japanese detainees were interned survive (16' × 30', now vacation cabins).
- KMC information in the report came from NPS / KMC material.

### NARA Access to Archival Databases (AAD) — Japanese-American Internee Data File, 1942–1946

## Q5 — Huntington Beach / Orange County — DOCUMENTED local history

This is the strongest local paper in the pull.

### Civilian Exclusion Order No. 61 — the Orange County order

**Primary poster text — CSUDH digital collections:**
http://digitalcollections.archives.csudh.edu/digital/api/collection/p16855coll4/id/5899/download

- **Civilian Exclusion Order No. 61, May 10, 1942** (Western Defense Command / Wartime Civil Control Administration, signed J. L. DeWitt) — the order covering Orange County.
- Area described: from Bolsa Chica Ave / Westminster Blvd through Santa Ana limits, Hwy 101 to Laguna Beach, shoreline back to Bolsa Chica, "together with all parcels of Orange County not heretofore covered."
- **All persons of Japanese ancestry, alien and non-alien, were to be evacuated by 12 noon Sunday, May 17, 1942.**
- No residence change after noon May 10, 1942 without special permission from the Civil Control Station at **Memorial Hall, Sixth and Magnolia Streets, Huntington Beach, California**.
- Permits were allowed only to unite families or in grave emergency.

**City of Huntington Beach records (File #22-426, May 17, 2022):**
https://records.huntingtonbeachca.gov/WebLink/DocView.aspx?id=6269029&dbid=0&repo=COHB

- May 17 was the mandatory deadline for all persons of Japanese ancestry to be forcibly removed from Orange County per Civilian Exclusion Order No. 61, including the Wintersburg Village and Huntington Beach areas.
- They **reported to the Pacific Electric Railway station next to the Huntington Beach pier and were transported to Poston (Colorado River Relocation Center, near Parker, Arizona).**

**Discover Nikkei (a Japanese American National Museum project), Mary Adams Urashima, "Moving Day," June 21, 2017:**
https://discovernikkei.org/en/journal/2017/6/21/6731/

- Orange County "moving day" was May 17, 1942.
- The *Santa Ana Register* (May 18, 1942) reported **1,543 people from Orange County** now at the camp near Parker Dam, Arizona.
- Civilian Exclusion Order No. 61 covered the region including Wintersburg Village and Huntington Beach.
- Registration took place the prior week at Civil Control Stations, with family ID numbers assigned.

### Historic Wintersburg — Huntington Beach

Location: Warner Ave / Nichols Lane, north Huntington Beach. The Furuta family farm + Wintersburg Japanese Presbyterian Mission/Church.

Documented facts (City of Huntington Beach records, File #22-426, and preservation sources below):

- Charles Mitsuji Furuta arrived from Hiroshima-ken.
- Land was purchased in 1908 by Rev. Hisakichi Terasawa and Furuta, and deeded to Furuta in 1912 — rare Issei-owned land acquired **before** the California Alien Land Laws of 1913/1920.
- The mission was founded in 1904 — Orange County's first Japanese church.
- Six structures: 1910 Mission, 1910 Manse, 1934 Church, 1912 Furuta bungalow, c.1908–1912 barn, 1947 ranch house, on ~4.4–5 acres.
- A fire on February 25, 2022 destroyed the two oldest mission structures.
- **World War II:** Charles Furuta (landowner, president of the Japanese Association) was taken by the FBI first to **Tuna Canyon Detention Station**, then to the **DOJ enemy-alien camp at Lordsburg, New Mexico**. **Yukiko Furuta and the children were removed from Wintersburg on May 16, 1942 to Poston.** The family was separated for one year, reunited at Poston, confined three years, and released to return to the farm in 1945.
- In Huntington Beach generally: most removed residents gathered at the Pacific Electric station near the pier; others went via the Japanese Language School in Garden Grove; they were bused to Poston. **575 Japanese Americans in the area were required to register in downtown Huntington Beach** (per Preserve Orange County).
- City records: Wintersburg was the center of the Japanese community in Orange County in the early 1900s; the site was identified as a historical cultural landmark in the City's 1973 Open Space/Conservation report and in the 1996 General Plan; NPS inspected in 2013 and determined the property **eligible for the National Register of Historic Places**; it was named one of America's 11 Most Endangered Historic Places (National Trust, June 2014) and a **National Treasure (National Trust, October 2015)**; City Council created the Historic Wintersburg Preservation Task Force in July 2012.
- November 2025: the California State Historic Resources Commission recommended Wintersburg for the National Register, and it is now listed on the **California Register of Historical Resources**. The owner, Republic Services, objected, so NPS cannot formally list it on the National Register but can determine eligibility (per Preserve Orange County).
